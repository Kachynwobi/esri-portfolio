01 · The Product

ArcGIS GeoBIM joins two kinds of data that rarely meet.

Autodesk tools hold the detailed models that architects and engineers build for a single structure. ArcGIS holds the geospatial systems that place that structure in the real world. GeoBIM brings them into one web experience, so a project carries both its design detail and its location at once. My role was to make that experience learnable through content, on a platform used by 700,000+ customer organizations, for people who could not call support every time a term surprised them.

02 · The Problem

Two readers, two languages.

GeoBIM serves two core audiences. Autodesk and BIM professionals think in buildings, levels, and elements. GIS professionals think in maps, layers, and coordinate systems. Each group is expert in its own domain and a newcomer in the other, and the same word often carries two meanings depending on who is reading it.

Content that served one audience cleanly could quietly lose the other. The writing had to hold both mental models at once and take either reader to a real first success without a translator in the room.

05 · Inside the Craft

The decisions behind the words

A clean surface hides a set of deliberate calls. Five shaped nearly every page.

01

Map the reader before the feature

Every decision started from who was reading and what they were trying to do, not from what the software could technically do. A GIS analyst adding a building and an architect publishing a model arrive with different goals and different next steps. The content followed the task, not the interface.

02

Reconcile the vocabulary

The fastest way to lose a reader is a word that means something else in their world. I built a shared reference for the terms that collide across Autodesk and GIS, and made deliberate calls about when to lead with a reader's own vocabulary and when to name the other side's term. Consistency became a design decision.

03

Layer the detail

Complex products fail when they explain everything at once. The sequence kept the essential path short and held the depth for anyone who needed it. Getting-started content earned a first win quickly. Reference content carried the full picture for the moment a reader went deeper. Progressive disclosure carried the load.

04

Build content as a system, not as pages

I authored in a structured, single-source approach so a concept was defined once and reused everywhere it appeared. That kept a fast-moving product consistent, kept updates inexpensive, and meant one change propagated cleanly instead of drifting across dozens of pages. The content model did the work.

05

Write for the moment it breaks

The most important words in any product are the ones a person reads when something is not working. I treated error states, empty states, and edge cases as first-class content, telling the reader what happened and what to do next in plain language and in the product's voice. The recovery path was designed, not left to chance.

06 · Terminology

Registering two vocabularies

In GeoBIM, two datasets only work together once they are registered to a shared coordinate frame. The content faced the same task with language. Below is a sample of the words that carry two meanings, and the content decision that resolved each.

Term
In the BIM world
In the GIS world
Content decision
Model
The building itself, authored in tools like Revit
A data or geoprocessing model
Lead with the reader's meaning, name the other explicitly on first cross-reference
Layer
A drawing or discipline layer
A map layer of features
Anchor to the map layer as the product's primary sense, flag the BIM sense inside AEC workflows
Level
A building storey
A scale or zoom level
Reserve "level" for the storey, use "scale" for the map sense
Feature
A capability, or a building element
A geographic record: point, line, polygon
Use "feature" only in its GIS sense, say "capability" or "element" everywhere else
Coordinate system
Local project coordinates, often arbitrary
A real-world spatial reference
Teach georeferencing as the hinge concept that joins the two worlds

07 · The Through-Line

The same discipline, a different surface

Documentation and conversation design are one craft in different clothing. Both start from user intent. Both meet a person inside their own mental model instead of the system's. Both guide through complexity with the fewest words that will do the job. And both live or die on the moment of confusion, when someone is stuck and the next line of copy decides whether they recover or leave.

Anticipating what the reader is trying to do
→
Modeling user intent turn by turn
Meeting the reader's mental model
→
Designing for context and persona
An onboarding sequence with one win per step
→
Layered responses that never overload a turn
Terminology reconciliation
→
Controlled vocabulary and entity design
Single-source content system
→
Reusable conversational components behind the interface
Microcopy for error and empty states
→
Fallback, repair, and recovery flows

The GeoBIM work was documentation on the surface. Underneath, it was intent modeling, vocabulary design, progressive disclosure, and repair. That is conversation design.

08 · The Outcome

What it produced

  • A four-part getting-started experience that takes a brand-new user from first look to published, shared work.
  • Published, co-authored technical articles that extended the documentation into advanced, real-world GIS and BIM workflows.
  • A shared, structured vocabulary that kept a fast-moving product coherent across both audiences.
  • A single-source content system that made consistency the default and updates inexpensive.
Why it matters for what comes next

The instinct that runs through this work, start from intent, respect the reader's language, reveal complexity in the right order, and design carefully for the moment things break, is the same instinct conversation design runs on. I carry it into Aura by Paula, my pre-launch styling platform, where those principles shape how a conversational AI meets a person it is still learning about.
